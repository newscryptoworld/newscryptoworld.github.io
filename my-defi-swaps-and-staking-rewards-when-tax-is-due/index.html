My DeFi swaps and staking rewards: when tax is due

How are my DeFi swaps and staking rewards taxed? Swaps can trigger capital gains or losses; staking rewards are usually income at their market value when received.

Crypto Record EditorialNov 19, 2026 · 03:00 UTC4 min read

DeFi tax rules help you classify each swap and reward before you work out what to report. In the US and UK, crypto is generally treated as property for tax purposes, so swapping one token for another can count as a disposal even when no dollars change hands.

When does a DeFi swap count as a taxable disposal?

A token-to-token swap is generally a disposal of the token you give up. Work out its value in your local currency at the time of the transaction, then compare that value with your cost basis: the amount you paid, adjusted for relevant costs.

Say you swap SHIB for BONE on shiba swap. ShibaSwap is a decentralized exchange where users swap tokens through smart contracts. Your gain or loss on the SHIB disposal is the value of BONE received minus the cost basis of the SHIB you gave up. The BONE’s value at receipt generally becomes its starting basis for a later disposal.

Keep the swap and its gas fee as separate tax details. If the network fee is paid in ETH, spending that ETH is itself a small disposal: compare the ETH’s value when used for gas with its cost basis. The fee is also a transaction cost for the swap. Depending on the country’s rules and how the fee relates to the trade, it can reduce the disposal proceeds or add to the acquired token’s cost basis. Record the fee’s value and currency so you can apply the right treatment.

The same basic idea applies to swaps on other protocols. In the UK, HMRC treats exchanging one type of cryptoasset for another as a disposal for Capital Gains Tax. In the US, the IRS treats digital assets as property, and exchanging one for another can produce a capital gain or loss. The exact calculation and reporting rules differ by country.

When are staking rewards income, and what happens when you sell?

Staking rewards are generally income when you receive control of the tokens, measured at their market value at that time. For US taxpayers, IRS Revenue Ruling 2023-14 says staking rewards are included in income when the taxpayer gains dominion and control. In the UK, HMRC says awarded tokens are generally taxable as miscellaneous income at their pound sterling value when received, unless the activity amounts to a trade.

Take BONE earned through Bury, ShibaSwap’s staking feature. Record how many tokens arrived, when they became available to you, and their market value then. That value is generally both the income amount and the BONE’s cost basis for a later sale or swap.

A later disposal is a second tax event. If you sell or swap those BONE tokens, compare the proceeds with the basis already recorded as income. If their value has risen, the difference may be a capital gain; if it has fallen, it may be a capital loss, subject to local rules.

For locked rewards, timing depends on whether you can access or control them. Under the US rule, rewards that are merely accruing and cannot be sold, exchanged or transferred generally are not income until you gain control. Note when any lock ends and when the tokens become available. UK treatment also turns on receipt, so check when the tokens are actually awarded and accessible.

Liquidity pool deposits and withdrawals need their own records. Their tax treatment can depend on the tokens and rights involved, and remains unsettled in some situations. Record each asset deposited, each asset returned, timestamps, values and fees, and treat the transaction as a possible disposal until you have determined the applicable rule.

Related

Do I owe tax if I never cashed out to dollars?

Possibly. A token-to-token swap can create a taxable disposal even if you keep all value in crypto and never withdraw dollars. A reward can also count as income when received, depending on your country’s rules.

How do I find the cost basis of tokens bought years ago?

Start with exchange statements, wallet history and transaction records. Match each purchase to its date, quantity, local-currency value and fees. US rules may allow specific identification when your records support it; UK calculations generally use token pools and matching rules, so a single remembered purchase price may not be enough.
