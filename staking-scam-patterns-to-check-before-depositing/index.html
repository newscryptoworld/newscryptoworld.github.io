Staking scam patterns to check before depositing

Staking scams to watch for before depositing include lookalike sites, guaranteed high yields, and fake support. Check the domain and never share your seed phrase.

Crypto Record EditorialNov 20, 2026 · 21:00 UTC4 min read

Before depositing, check the site address, reject platforms promising fixed high yields, and ignore support messages asking for your seed phrase.

In real staking, you use your own wallet to send a deposit to a protocol contract. The contract connects the deposit to the staking process; a scam can instead take your funds before any staking happens. A quick check of the domain, the promised return, and any support request can catch the main warning signs.

Sponsored search ads can appear above the real staking site and lead to a lookalike domain. EigenLayer is an Ethereum restaking protocol that lets stakers reuse staked ETH or liquid staking tokens to help secure additional services. Save the protocol’s official site as a bookmark from its official docs, then open the staking app from that bookmark. This is especially useful when a search ad or a message makes a site look urgent or familiar. The three checks below take about a minute before you deposit.

Lookalike sites and fake claim pages

A lookalike staking site copies a real protocol’s name and design to get you to connect a wallet or approve a transaction. A fake EigenLayer points or rewards claim page may use a similar trick, presenting a claim as the next step when it is really asking for wallet access or a transfer. This check suits anyone arriving at a staking app through a search result, ad, or social message.

Read the full domain in the address bar and compare it with the address saved from the protocol’s official docs. A familiar logo or page layout is not enough: a phishing domain can change a small part of the name while keeping the rest recognizable. Type the URL from your saved bookmark; do not follow an ad or DM link to start a deposit or claim.

Before approving a wallet prompt, make sure you are still on the bookmarked site. If a claim page appeared from a new link, close it and return through the bookmark. A real staking deposit is sent from your wallet to a protocol contract, so a page that asks you to send funds to a different address deserves a fresh check.

Guaranteed-yield platforms that hold your funds

A guaranteed-yield platform may promise a fixed, unusually high return and ask you to send ETH to an address it controls. That arrangement gives the platform custody of your funds; it is not the usual wallet-to-protocol-contract staking flow. This check suits anyone comparing a staking offer with the yield available through a protocol.

Ethereum staking yield varies with network activity and the number of validators, so it is not a fixed rate. A service can describe an estimated or changing return, but a promise that your rate is guaranteed should prompt you to ask who controls the deposited ETH and how it reaches staking. If the platform requires a transfer to its own address, do not treat the promised rate as proof that your ETH is being staked.

Compare the deposit steps with the protocol’s official information. If the address belongs to the platform and the platform holds the funds, you are relying on that operator to return them. Pause before sending ETH and confirm the custody arrangement instead of judging the offer by its headline yield.

Impersonated support asking for your seed phrase

Fake support accounts contact stakers in Discord or Telegram and ask for a seed phrase or a “wallet sync” to fix a deposit or claim. This check suits anyone who gets an unexpected message while using a staking service. Real teams do not DM first, and no legitimate service needs your seed phrase.

Your seed phrase gives access to your wallet, so do not type it into a support form, share it in a chat, or enter it into a page sent by a stranger. A “wallet sync” request from an unsolicited DM is another reason to stop. Close the conversation and reach support through the contact route listed on the protocol’s official site.

Before your next deposit, open the saved bookmark, check where the funds are going, and dismiss unsolicited support messages. That minute-long routine can help you spot a fake site, a custody offer dressed as staking, or an attempt to take control of your wallet.
