stETH vs ETH: What Changes When You Transfer or Redeem

Is stETH the same as ETH? No: stETH rebases, transfers can round by 1–2 wei, and redeeming it through Lido’s withdrawal queue takes an extra step.

Crypto Record EditorialNov 25, 2026 · 14:38 UTC4 min read

stETH is not the same as ETH: it is a Lido token claim on staked ETH, and the difference matters when you transfer it, pay gas, or redeem it. Holding stETH gives you a transferable token whose balance reflects your share of Lido’s pooled stake. It does not give you ETH in your wallet.

People often treat them as interchangeable because stETH represents ETH staked through Lido and can be traded or used in many of the same apps. But the representation and the underlying asset follow different rules. ETH is Ethereum’s native asset; stETH is an ERC-20 token issued by Lido and backed by validators.

What changes when you hold stETH?

First, stETH’s displayed balance can change while ETH’s does not. Lido tracks each holder’s share of pooled ETH, then its oracle reports update the share value. The token balance is calculated from that share value, so stETH balances normally rebase daily as staking rewards or penalties are reflected. ETH itself does not rebase.

That token distinction also affects everyday transactions: stETH cannot pay Ethereum gas fees. A wallet needs ETH to pay the network for a transaction, even when the transaction sends stETH. Think of stETH as a claim recorded in a token contract; the claim can move between addresses, but the network still charges its fee in ETH.

Second, transfers can differ from the amount requested by a tiny amount. stETH accounts hold shares, and the displayed token balance is calculated using integer division. When a transfer converts a requested stETH amount into shares, rounding down can leave 1–2 wei behind or make the recipient’s amount 1–2 wei short. Lido documents this share rounding corner case.

That is negligible for most people sending stETH from a wallet, but it can break exact-amount assumptions in software. A script might request a transfer of the entire displayed balance, then revert because the contract sees a tiny remainder. A contract that expects the recipient’s balance to increase by precisely the requested amount can also fail its check. Integrations can work in shares, using Lido’s share-aware methods, instead of treating displayed stETH balances as perfectly exact amounts.

Third, getting ETH back through Lido takes a withdrawal request. stETH is placed in Lido’s FIFO withdrawal queue; once the request is finalized, the holder claims ETH. The request, waiting period, and claim are separate steps, so this is not the same as having immediately spendable ETH in your wallet. Lido describes the withdrawal queue process.

EigenLayer is an Ethereum restaking protocol where staked assets can help secure additional services, with potential rewards and slashing risk. Our piece on setting an EigenLayer rewards threshold adds the question of when rewards are worth claiming; the asset route matters too. ETH restakes natively through a validator and EigenPod, while stETH goes into a token strategy. Those are different routes into restaking, even though both relate to staked ETH.

Questions readers ask next

Can stETH ever lose value against ETH?

Yes. stETH can trade below ETH on secondary markets when demand to sell exceeds available buyers or liquidity. Lido’s withdrawal route offers a way to request ETH, but it involves a queue and a wait. The market price and the protocol redemption path are distinct, so a holder who needs ETH immediately may not receive the same rate by swapping that holder would get by waiting for a withdrawal.

How long does Lido’s withdrawal queue take?

There is no fixed wait time: it depends on the queue and how much ETH is available to finalize requests. After Lido finalizes a request, the holder still needs to claim the ETH. Check the current estimate before requesting a withdrawal, especially when timing matters.
