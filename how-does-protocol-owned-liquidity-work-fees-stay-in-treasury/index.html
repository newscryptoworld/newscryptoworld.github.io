How does protocol-owned liquidity work: Fees stay in treasury

How protocol-owned liquidity works in DeFi: a treasury supplies a pool, holds the LP tokens, earns fees and marks the position to market each period.

Crypto Record EditorialNov 27, 2026 · 10:33 UTC5 min read

Protocol-owned liquidity (POL) works by having a protocol’s treasury deposit assets into a decentralized exchange pool and hold the liquidity-provider (LP) tokens, keeping the liquidity under protocol control while collecting its share of trading fees. Unlike liquidity mining, which rents depth with rewards that can leave when incentives end, POL gives the treasury a position to manage over time. Fraxswap’s fraxswap liquidity adds a time-weighted market maker that can help a treasury acquire assets gradually. The OlympusDAO explanation of POL and Frax’s Fraxswap overview describe how protocols use and manage these mechanisms.

  • You’ll be able to outline how a treasury acquires, holds and accounts for POL.
  • It takes a governance-approved asset pair, a pool deposit and recurring records of the LP position’s value.

How a treasury turns assets into protocol-owned liquidity

Say a DAO wants a deeper market for its governance token against a stablecoin. It approves a deposit of both assets into a pool on Curve or Uniswap. In return, the pool issues LP tokens representing the treasury’s share of the pool. The protocol keeps those tokens in treasury custody, instead of distributing them to liquidity providers as rewards.

Those LP tokens are the position the DAO owns; the corresponding pool share contains both assets and changes in value as traders swap. Trading fees accrue to liquidity providers according to the pool’s rules, so the treasury receives its share while it holds the position. The resulting liquidity can remain available to traders without the protocol continually paying incentives to attract outside LPs. It is like owning a shop’s inventory rather than paying a supplier to keep stock on the shelf: the owner retains the asset and bears the change in its value.

The pool share can shift in composition as prices move. If the token’s price rises or falls relative to the stablecoin, the pool’s balancing mechanism changes how much of each asset is represented by the LP position. That creates impermanent loss: the position can be worth less than simply holding the original assets. It is a real treasury exposure even when the protocol has not withdrawn the liquidity. Fees may offset some of that effect, but they do not remove the need to measure it.

A treasury can source the deposit from existing reserves, or acquire assets through bonding. OlympusDAO popularized acquiring POL through bonds, which can exchange assets for LP tokens or other treasury assets under the bond mechanism. A protocol can also build a position gradually. Frax built Fraxswap partly to support its own POL and treasury operations; Fraxswap TWAMM allows large trades to be spread over time, which can help a treasury accumulate the second asset in stages rather than making one large market order.

Operations should start with governance approval that records the intended pair, deposit amounts, custody address and authority to rebalance or withdraw. At each reporting period, record the pool and pair address, LP-token balance, underlying asset amounts, and a mark-to-market valuation using that period’s prices. Include fees received and any deposits, withdrawals or rebalances. The accounting value should reflect the current position, not merely the original cost basis, because both the pool’s composition and asset prices can change.

With those controls in place, the DAO can compare fee income and liquidity depth with the position’s changing market value and risk. The operating decision is whether the treasury can sustain the exposure and reporting cadence for the pair it wants to support.

Related questions

Is protocol-owned liquidity better than liquidity mining?

Protocol-owned liquidity can be more durable because the treasury retains the LP tokens after incentives end. Liquidity mining can attract outside capital quickly, but providers may leave when rewards stop. POL is not automatically better: the protocol commits treasury assets and takes on pool risk, while mining shifts the position to external LPs at an ongoing incentive cost.

What are the risks of protocol-owned liquidity?

The main risks include impermanent loss, price declines in either asset, smart-contract or pool failure, and reduced flexibility if assets are needed elsewhere. A position can also change composition as prices move, so the treasury should track its underlying exposure rather than treating LP tokens as fixed-value cash.

Putting POL into treasury practice

How should a DAO account for POL in treasury reports?

Report the LP-token balance and pair address, then value the position at the reporting date using its underlying asset quantities and current prices. Record fee income, deposits, withdrawals and rebalances separately. This mark-to-market view shows how the position changed during the period; cost basis alone cannot show its current value or asset mix.

Can POL be withdrawn quickly in a crisis?

A DAO can generally withdraw by redeeming its LP tokens through the pool, subject to the pool’s mechanics and transaction execution. The result is the pool’s then-current mix of assets, not necessarily the original deposit. Governance, custody controls, market depth and the pool’s condition can all affect how useful that withdrawal is in a crisis, so the treasury should decide in advance who can authorize it and how the resulting assets will be handled.
