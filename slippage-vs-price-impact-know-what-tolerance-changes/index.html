Slippage vs price impact: Know what tolerance changes

Slippage vs price impact difference: price impact is in the quote; tolerance limits later execution changes. Learn which setting can change the swap result.

Crypto Record EditorialNov 28, 2026 · 06:09 UTC4 min read

Slippage vs price impact describes two different parts of a swap: price impact is the change your trade causes and is already reflected in the quote, while slippage is a further change before execution that your tolerance caps. Pool depth sets price impact; time and other trades can change the execution price and cause slippage.

That distinction matters when a quote spans more than one chain. LI.FI is a cross-chain bridge aggregator that helps readers find routes for moving crypto between blockchains. A related question is bridging Arbitrum to Optimism with ETH or USDC. In a route that includes swaps, price impact and tolerance apply to those swap legs; a leg that only moves the same token across chains has no swap price impact.

What changes the quote, and what tolerance controls

Price impact changes the quoted output; slippage tolerance limits how much worse execution may be after that quote. An AMM prices a trade against the liquidity available in its pool. In a constant-product pool such as Uniswap v2, the reserves follow x·y=k: as a trade takes tokens from one reserve and adds them to the other, the pool’s price moves along that curve.

The trade size relative to the reserves determines how far along the curve the swap moves. A large order against a thin pool shifts the price more than a small order against a deep pool. That effect is estimated when the quote is made, so the quoted output already reflects the trade’s price impact.

Picture pushing a paddle through a small pool of water: the paddle displaces more of the available water than it would in a lake. In an AMM, the pool’s reserves play the role of the available water, and the swap changes the balance used to price the next tokens.

Slippage describes a later change between the quoted price and execution. The pool can change while a transaction waits to be included: another trader may swap first, or the market may move. Slippage tolerance is the maximum worsening you accept relative to the quote. For an exact-input swap, it sets a minimum received amount. If execution would deliver less than that minimum, the swap reverts.

Say a hypothetical swap has a reference value of $10,000, but the pool quote is worth $9,700 after price impact. With a 1% tolerance, the minimum received is $9,603, ignoring fees for simplicity. The quote still says $9,700; tolerance does not improve it. If the pool changes before execution and the output drops below $9,603, the transaction fails instead of completing at that lower amount.

How to read the difference in a cross-chain route

In a lifi bridge route, slippage tolerance sets the minimum received on swap legs, while each leg’s price impact is part of its quoted output. A route may have a swap before bridging, after bridging, or both. Each swap interacts with its own pool liquidity; a same-token bridge leg does not trade against a pool and therefore has no price impact.

When a quote shows high price impact, raising tolerance only makes a revert less likely by accepting a lower possible output. It does not reduce the trade’s impact or change the quoted amount. To lower price impact, reduce the trade size or use a route through deeper liquidity. To limit execution risk after quoting, choose a tolerance that reflects how much output you are willing to give up if conditions change.

Read the quoted output first, then compare the minimum received with the amount you would accept. Reduce the swap or seek deeper liquidity to address high price impact; adjust tolerance only to set your execution floor.
