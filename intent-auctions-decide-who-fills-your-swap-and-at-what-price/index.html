Intent Auctions Decide Who Fills Your Swap and at What Price

How do solvers compete to fill my intent-based order? They bid under auction rules to execute your signed minimum, shaping both the price you receive and when it fills.

Crypto Record EditorialNov 18, 2026 · 10:49 UTC4 min read

An intent-based swap order lets you set the amount you will sell and the minimum you will accept, then lets solvers compete to execute it. Say you sign an EIP-712 order to sell 5,000 USDC for at least 1.9 ETH. Solvers can submit different ways to fill it, but the winning settlement must deliver at least 1.9 ETH.

A routed swap asks a trading interface to find a route across liquidity sources for a transaction you send. A Matcha swap is useful when you want that routed-swap flow. Matcha Auto also starts from a signed order; for the distinction between these approaches, see how does matcha swap work.

How the auction shapes your fill

After you sign, the order is an off-chain EIP-712 message until a solver or resolver wins and submits its settlement on-chain. The message specifies what you will sell and the minimum output you will accept. The auction rules determine how competing fills are compared and when one can proceed.

A solver can route the order through AMMs, use its own token inventory, or match it directly with an opposite order. That last route is a Coincidence of Wants: another trader wants to sell what you want to buy, and wants what you are selling. The solver compares available routes and submits a solution under the auction’s rules.

CoW Swap uses batch auctions. Solvers submit competing solutions, and the auction ranks them by the surplus delivered to you. Surplus is the value beyond the order’s required minimum. A solver that can secure a better outcome for your order has a stronger chance of winning.

1inch Fusion uses a Dutch auction. The offered rate starts above market and decays over time. Resolvers watch the order and fill when the rate becomes profitable for them. The first profitable resolver to take the order wins, so the rate at fill time depends on how quickly one accepts it.

Those designs can produce different outcomes for the same signed order. Take the 5,000 USDC order under the same market conditions. In a CoW Swap batch, several solvers submit together, and the solution giving you the most surplus wins. In a 1inch Fusion Dutch auction, resolvers wait as the rate decays; the first one willing to fill at the current rate takes it. The deciding factor is the auction design: one compares submitted solutions, while the other makes timing part of the competition.

Solvers and resolvers compete because a winning fill can earn them value from the trade, after accounting for the route and execution costs. They pay the gas to settle the order and recover that cost from the trade. You sign the order without holding gas for that specific settlement, while the competition can push solvers to pass a better price back to you.

To judge a fill, compare its expected output with your signed minimum and consider whether the auction’s timing fits your needs. The minimum sets your floor; the auction design determines how offers compete above it and when the order may execute.

Related questions

Can a solver fill my order at a worse price than I signed?

No. The settlement must deliver at least the minimum output in your signed order. A solver can win by delivering more, but cannot validly settle below that floor.

Who pays gas on an intent order?

The winning solver or resolver pays the gas for settlement and recovers it from the trade. You do not need to hold gas for that order’s settlement.

What to ask next

What happens to my order if no solver fills it before it expires?

If the order expires unfilled, it can no longer be settled under its terms. Your tokens remain yours, and you can submit a new order if you still want to trade.

How do I cancel a signed intent order?

Cancellation depends on the protocol and order type. Use that protocol’s cancellation method; simply signing an order does not itself explain how to revoke it.

Are intent orders protected from sandwich attacks?

Protection depends on the protocol’s settlement design and how the order is executed. Check the protocol’s specific protections and the terms of your order before signing.
