How Much Gas Should You Keep on the Destination Chain?

Keep enough of the destination chain’s native token for the first action after a bridge; the right reserve depends on the route and transaction.

Crypto Record EditorialOct 21, 2026 · 16:50 UTC4 min read

Keep enough of the destination chain’s native token to pay for your first planned transaction after the bridge. There is no universal reserve: the amount depends on what you will do, which route you use, and the fee market when you act.

A bridge moves an asset between networks through a route-specific process. You approve or send a transaction on the source chain, the bridge processes it, and the destination chain credits or releases the asset. Some routes may also require a separate claim transaction; others handle delivery for you. The Polygon Bridge route guide covers how route choices affect the transfer. The destination gas question is separate: what can you do once the asset arrives?

Why do I need gas after bridging?

You need the destination chain’s gas token to submit transactions there. A wallet can hold a bridged token and still be unable to swap, send, or deposit it if the wallet has no native gas token. The token you bridge does not necessarily pay that network fee.

Think of the bridged asset as a package delivered to a new building. The destination gas token is the fare for the next trip from there. The bridge can deliver the package, but it does not automatically fund every action you might want to take afterward.

Gas measures the computation a transaction uses, and the fee also reflects the network’s price per unit of gas. A simple transfer and a contract interaction can use different amounts. The fee can change as network demand changes, so a balance that covered one transaction yesterday may not cover the same action later.

How do I estimate the amount to keep?

Start with the first action you intend to take, then use the wallet or app’s fee estimate on the destination network. Add up the likely fees for the actions you expect to make before you can obtain more gas. Leave some extra room for changing fees or a transaction that costs more than the estimate.

For example, if you plan to swap a bridged token and then send the proceeds, budget for both transactions. If you only plan to hold the asset, you may not need a large working balance immediately. But keep enough for any destination claim that the route requires; check the bridge’s transaction status and instructions to see whether that step applies.

  • Identify the exact destination network and its native gas token.
  • List the actions you plan to take after the funds arrive.
  • Check the fee estimate for each action in the wallet or app you will use.
  • Keep a buffer, and reassess if the network is busy or your plan changes.

Do not treat a displayed fee as a fixed quote. The wallet estimates the transaction’s gas use and the current fee conditions; the final amount can differ. A larger balance is not automatically better, though. Keeping a modest reserve for planned actions limits the amount of native token sitting idle while still giving you room to use the bridged funds.

What if the bridged funds arrive but I have no gas?

Your tokens may be visible in the wallet but remain unusable for on-chain actions until you add the destination chain’s gas token. That does not by itself mean the bridge transfer failed. Confirm the transaction reached the intended network, then arrange a small gas top-up through a trusted route or service available to you.

Before sending, verify the destination network, wallet address, token contract, and fee token shown by the wallet. A token with the same name can exist on multiple networks, and a bridge route determines which version you receive. The practical rule is simple: budget gas for the destination steps you actually expect to take, with a buffer, and keep that reserve in the token the destination network accepts for fees.
