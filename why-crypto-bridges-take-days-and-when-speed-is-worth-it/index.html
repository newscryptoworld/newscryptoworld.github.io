Why Crypto Bridges Take Days, and When Speed Is Worth It

A bridge’s quoted time depends on how it verifies and settles transfers. Learn what can take days, what faster routes change, and how to choose.

Crypto Record EditorialOct 20, 2026 · 14:01 UTC4 min read

A crypto bridge can take days because it may wait for the source chain to finalize a transfer before releasing funds on the destination chain. The right settlement speed depends on what the bridge waits for, whether another party advances the destination funds, and how much delay you can accept.

Start with the transfer’s path. A user sends tokens to a contract on the source chain. The bridge’s validator or relayer watches for that transaction, checks that it meets the route’s rules, then sends or authorizes a corresponding action on the destination chain. A contract there releases tokens, mints a representation, or pays out funds already supplied by a liquidity provider. Each step has its own wait.

Some routes also require a period for other participants to challenge a message before it is treated as valid. That waiting period can stretch a transfer from minutes to days. The linked mayan bridge article goes deeper on this subject; the key distinction here is whether a route waits for settlement or pays out before it.

Why can a crypto bridge take days?

A bridge takes days when its settlement rules include a long verification or challenge period. A transaction first needs to be included in a block on the source chain. The bridge then waits for the required evidence of that transaction, which can mean additional confirmations or a period during which a disputed message can be challenged. Only after that condition is met does the destination contract release funds.

This delay is separate from the time it takes to submit a transaction. A wallet may show the source transaction as confirmed while the bridge is still waiting to complete its own checks. Network congestion can add time at either end, but it does not explain every multi-day wait; the route’s verification design may be the main cause.

Think of it like a bank transfer that waits for a document check before paying out. The document check is the bridge’s verification step, while the destination payment is settlement. The analogy stops there: on-chain transfers follow contract rules, and the user should check the route’s stated process rather than assume a bank-like dispute system.

What changes on a faster bridge route?

A faster route can pay the destination side before the original transfer has completed its full settlement process. A liquidity provider, sometimes called a solver, supplies funds on the destination chain after checking the request. The bridge later reconciles that advance against the source-chain transaction.

This changes who waits. The user may receive destination funds sooner, while the provider waits for the underlying transfer to settle. Speed comes from available liquidity and the route’s rules; it does not mean the source chain has finalized sooner. If liquidity is unavailable, a fast route may be delayed or may not be offered for that transfer.

Before choosing, compare the route’s actual terms:

  • Quoted completion time: Check whether it describes arrival on the destination chain or full settlement.
  • Transfer cost: Include network fees and any route fee in the amount you expect to receive.
  • Available amount: A fast route may have limits tied to its available liquidity.
  • What happens if delayed: Confirm how the route handles a transaction that is pending or cannot complete.

How should you choose a settlement speed?

Choose the slower route when the funds can wait and you prefer a route that completes its verification period before releasing the destination funds. Choose a faster route when timing matters and its fee, limits, and settlement terms suit the transfer. The better choice for most ordinary transfers is the least costly route that arrives before you need the funds, because paying for speed has little value when there is no deadline.

Check that the source and destination networks match the route, and verify the token and receiving address before sending. A bridge transfer is not simply a faster version of a normal wallet send: it relies on contracts and messages across networks, so a mistake can be difficult to reverse. Once you submit, use the bridge’s transaction status and the destination chain’s explorer to see whether the source step, verification, and payout have each completed.
