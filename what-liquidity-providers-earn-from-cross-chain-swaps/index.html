What Liquidity Providers Earn From Cross-Chain Swaps

Liquidity providers earn a share of swap or transfer fees for making assets available across chains, but costs, inventory shifts and protocol design decide net returns.

Crypto Record EditorialOct 15, 2026 · 18:51 UTC4 min read

Liquidity providers earn fees for making tokens available to cross-chain swaps, but their take depends on the route and the costs of keeping liquidity ready. In a pool-based system, a provider deposits tokens into a contract. A user’s swap draws from that pool, and the contract collects a fee. In other systems, a relayer fronts tokens on the destination chain, then gets repaid from liquidity held elsewhere. The provider may earn a fee for supplying that capital, but does not necessarily collect every charge in the user’s quote.

Follow the transaction in order: the user selects a route, the source-chain contract receives or swaps the input token, and a bridge or messaging system carries instructions or proof of the transfer. The destination-chain contract releases or swaps the output token. The order book, pool or relayer determines where the assets come from; the settlement mechanism confirms who is owed what. For a first swap, see whether QuickSwap suits a first cross-chain swap. The choice of route affects the fee and the machinery behind it.

How do liquidity providers earn fees?

They earn a portion of the charges tied to the liquidity they supply. In an automated market maker, traders exchange against a pool of tokens. The contract adds the swap fee to the pool or distributes it according to the protocol’s rules. A provider’s share usually depends on their share of eligible liquidity while the trade occurs; supplying tokens does not guarantee a fixed payment.

Cross-chain routes can add a separate liquidity or transfer fee. In a pool-based bridge, the user’s input on one chain can be paid out from a pool on another. In an intent-based route, a relayer may front the destination tokens and later reclaim them through settlement. The fee breakdown can include compensation for LP capital as well as relayer costs, gas or a protocol charge. These are distinct roles, even when a system’s pricing combines them in one quoted amount.

Some protocols also offer token incentives to attract deposits. Those rewards are separate from fees generated by swaps. They can change over time, and their market value can move, so they should not be treated as a stable return.

What changes the amount an LP keeps?

Net earnings depend on fee revenue after costs and on what happens to the tokens in the pool. More routed volume can generate more fees, but it does not tell an LP how much they will earn: the provider’s share, the protocol’s fee split and the liquidity available all matter. A pool with little activity may produce little fee income even if its quoted rate looks attractive.

Cross-chain pools also face inventory imbalance. If users repeatedly take one token out of a destination pool, that pool can become short of it. The protocol may need deposits, rebalancing or other settlement to restore usable liquidity. A provider can end up holding a different mix of tokens than they supplied. In an AMM, price changes between the assets can also leave the provider worse off than simply holding them, a risk often called impermanent loss. Fees may offset that effect, but they do not erase it by definition.

  • Fee share: Check which charges go to LPs and which pay relayers, validators, gas or the protocol.
  • Utilization: Fees depend on actual routed swaps and transfers, not just the size of a pool.
  • Inventory: Look at how each chain’s pool is replenished and what happens when assets are unevenly used.
  • Withdrawal terms: Check whether liquidity can be removed at once or whether pending transfers or settlement delay it.

How should you compare LP returns?

Compare realized fee income with the risks and frictions of the specific pool. Start with the route’s fee rules, then check how fees are shared, how often liquidity is used, and whether the provider bears rebalancing or token-price risk. Include any withdrawal limits and the value of incentives separately from swap fees. A displayed annualized rate is a snapshot or estimate; it can change when volume, pool size, token prices or reward programs change.

The practical distinction is simple: an LP sells access to ready liquidity and earns according to the system’s fee rules. Cross-chain swaps make that service more involved because assets and settlement span networks. The better question is not just how much a route charges, but which actors receive each part and what the LP must risk or pay to keep liquidity available.
