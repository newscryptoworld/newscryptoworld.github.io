Same Ticker, Different Token: What a Bridge Actually Moves

A bridge can create or release a token with the same ticker as another asset; the chain, contract address and route determine what you actually hold.

Crypto Record EditorialOct 17, 2026 · 02:01 UTC4 min read

When you bridge a token, the destination asset may share the source token’s symbol without being the same token. A bridge moves value between networks by following a route: it may lock tokens on one chain and mint a corresponding token on another, or use liquidity already available on both sides. The ticker is a label. The chain and contract address identify the asset you receive.

In a lock-and-mint route, a bridge contract takes custody of the source tokens. A validator set, relayer network or other verification system confirms the deposit to the destination side. A destination contract then mints a token that represents a claim on the locked asset. For route-specific choices, the mantle bridge guide explains what to compare before transferring. Other routes draw from a pool of tokens on the destination chain, then settle balances across the networks.

Why can two tokens have the same symbol?

A symbol is a short display name stored in a token’s metadata; it is not a universal identity. Different contracts can each use the same symbol, just as different people can share a name. Wallets and trading interfaces often show the symbol because it is easy to scan, but the chain and contract address distinguish one asset from another.

That means a source token called ABC and a destination token called ABC may differ in who issued them, how they are backed and where they can be used. A destination token minted by a bridge may represent an asset locked elsewhere. A separate token with the same ticker may have no relationship to that bridge or the source asset at all. Even a familiar-looking name and logo do not establish that two contracts are equivalent.

What does the bridge do with the original tokens?

The route determines what happens to the source tokens. In a lock-and-mint design, the source contract holds them while a destination contract issues a corresponding amount. To reverse the transfer, the user sends the destination tokens to the bridge for burning; after that action is verified, the source contract releases the locked tokens.

A liquidity route works differently. The bridge or its liquidity providers send tokens already held on the destination chain, rather than minting a new representation for each transfer. The system later reconciles the amounts between chains. The user still receives an asset on the destination network, but it may be the chain’s existing token rather than a bridge-created version.

In either design, a transfer depends on several parts working together: the route’s contracts, the system that verifies activity across chains, and the destination mechanism that releases or supplies tokens. A bridge receipt or transaction record can show that a transfer completed, but the destination token’s identity still comes from its chain and contract.

How can you tell what you received?

Check the destination chain and the token contract address, then compare them with information from the bridge route or the asset’s issuer. Do this before trading, depositing or sending the token elsewhere. A ticker match alone cannot confirm that a token is the expected asset.

  • Confirm the destination network. The same contract address format can appear on different chains, where it refers to separate contracts.
  • Check the full token address. Match it against the route’s stated destination token or a trusted source.
  • Inspect the route model. Find out whether it mints a representation, releases an existing asset or uses destination liquidity.
  • Check where the token is accepted. A token with the expected symbol may still lack liquidity or support in the application you plan to use.

The practical takeaway is simple: bridging changes the network and may change the token contract, even when the symbol stays the same. Treat the ticker as a display label, and use the chain, address and route to decide what you actually hold.
