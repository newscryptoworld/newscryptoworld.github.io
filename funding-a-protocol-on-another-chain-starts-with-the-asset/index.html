Funding a Protocol on Another Chain Starts With the Asset

Funding a protocol on another chain means moving the right asset to its network, keeping gas for fees, then depositing through the protocol’s own contract.

Crypto Record EditorialOct 17, 2026 · 09:56 UTC4 min read

To fund a protocol on another chain, first get the required asset onto that chain, then use the protocol’s own deposit function. The process has two separate jobs: a bridge or exchange moves value between networks, and the protocol contract accepts that value for a specific purpose. Keeping those jobs distinct helps you choose the right asset, avoid a failed deposit and understand where your funds are at each step.

What do you need before moving funds?

Start on the protocol’s official app or documentation and check which network and asset its deposit screen accepts. A token’s name or ticker does not prove it is the right one: the same stablecoin symbol can refer to different contracts, and a protocol may accept only a particular version. Check the network shown in your wallet and the token contract or deposit details if the protocol provides them.

Next, check how you will obtain that asset on the destination chain. You might bridge a token you already hold, swap after bridging, or use an exchange that supports withdrawals to that network. These routes differ in fees, available assets and the number of transactions required. For a walkthrough of moving or swapping crypto with Acrossbridge, read the Acrossbridge guide to moving or swapping crypto. The choice of route does not change the protocol’s deposit requirements.

How does a bridge move crypto between chains?

A bridge coordinates a transfer between two networks, which do not share one common ledger. In a lock-and-mint design, the bridge contract locks a token on the source chain, then a corresponding token is issued on the destination chain. In a burn-and-release design, it destroys the source representation before releasing funds on the other chain. Some bridges instead use liquidity pools: the user sends one asset into a pool and receives an asset from a pool on the destination network.

In each case, the wallet signs a transaction on the source chain. The bridge or its validators confirm that transaction, and a second transaction completes the transfer on the destination chain. A pending transfer is not yet a protocol deposit; the destination asset must arrive in your wallet or be routed onward by the bridge interface.

Think of a bridge like a currency counter between two separate tills: it records what went in on one side and makes the corresponding value available on the other. The analogy stops there. The actual process depends on the bridge’s contracts, liquidity and verification design, and the asset received may be a wrapped or bridged version rather than the original token contract.

How do you deposit into the protocol?

Once the destination asset arrives, switch your wallet to the protocol’s network and open its deposit screen. Select the supported asset and amount, then read the transaction preview. The first transaction may approve a token contract to spend a stated amount; the next calls the protocol contract to supply, stake, lend or otherwise deposit it. Some interfaces combine or abstract these steps, but the wallet still asks you to authorize the resulting transactions.

Keep the network’s native token in your wallet for gas. You may need it to claim the bridge transfer, approve the asset and submit the deposit. Having exactly the amount you intend to deposit can leave you unable to complete the next transaction. The protocol may also require a minimum deposit or charge its own fee, so check the displayed terms before signing.

  • Confirm the destination network and accepted token.
  • Check the amount received after bridge fees or a swap.
  • Leave native tokens for destination-chain gas.
  • Verify the wallet transaction names the expected protocol and action.

For most users, the simpler route with fewer conversions is easier to verify, provided it delivers an asset the protocol accepts. After the deposit, check the protocol’s position or balance screen and confirm the transaction on the destination chain. A bridge transfer gets funds to a network; only the protocol’s deposit transaction puts them to work there.
